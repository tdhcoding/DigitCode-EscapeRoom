## 1. Purpose, scope, and inherited constraints

This document is the canonical planning contract for how a Ranked Match outcome
becomes a Ranked Rating change, and for what makes that change trustworthy. It
decides policy only. Schema, transport, and UI copy belong to the owners listed
in section 13.

The following were settled by earlier tickets and are inputs here, not
decisions:

- Ranked Match comes only from the Matchmaking Queue; a Player never chooses an
  Opponent. Waiting without a human Opponent falls back to a Bot Opponent.
  ([Chốt mô hình đối thủ của Ranked
  Match](https://github.com/tdhcoding/DigitCode-EscapeRoom/issues/25))
- Player-versus-Player results are zero-sum. Player-versus-Bot results move one
  rating only, so the system is hybrid. (the opponent-model decision Q10, Q11)
- Baseline rating is `1000`, `K` is `32`. (map Notes)
- Bot Opponent intensity tracks the Player's displayed Ranked Rating through
  `digitcode-bot-calibration/1.0.0`, which binds exactly one Ruleset and is
  calibrated on the domain `500..1500` with clamping outside it. ([Chốt hàm
  hiệu chuẩn Ranked Rating thành Score mục
  tiêu](https://github.com/tdhcoding/DigitCode-EscapeRoom/issues/30))
- Assignment, Queue Entry consumption, and Match Start are one atomic
  transaction; finalization is lazy, self-healing, atomic, and idempotent; a
  Player may not start a new Match before the previous one is finalized. ([Chốt
  Match lifecycle cho đường Matchmaking
  Queue](https://github.com/tdhcoding/DigitCode-EscapeRoom/issues/31))
- A running Match may not be cancelled, reset, or regenerated by anyone
  (R-T-08); a lost connection is never a Forfeit (R-T-07); the Match Clock
  never stops (R-S-04).
- Player Identity, not a session or device, carries Ranked Rating, Skill
  Estimate, and Match history. ([Chốt mức bảo đảm khi Player mất quyền truy
  cập](https://github.com/tdhcoding/DigitCode-EscapeRoom/issues/34))

## 2. No margin of victory

Ranked Rating MUST NOT depend on Score, Bot Score, or Solve Time. The score
pair fed to the rating formula is derived entirely from the Match outcome of
R-T-05: `S ∈ {0, 0.5, 1}`. Score and Solve Time act only as tiebreaks *inside*
R-T-05 to produce that outcome, and the rating formula never reads them.

The opponent-model decision Q7 attached a conditional constraint to this
ticket: if any rating term depended on Score or Solve Time, then R-P-13 (both
sides receive the same Puzzle) would become a value-transfer surface between
two accounts, and on the Bot Opponent edge that term would share an axis with
the calibration function `Ranked Rating -> target Score`. Choosing no margin
means the condition never fires. Three independent reasons:

1. The shared axis would be a feedback loop, not a risk. The profile sets the
   Bot Opponent's median Bot Score from the Player's own Ranked Rating, so a
   margin term would read the very number the profile moved to track the
   Player.
2. The pair cooldown of section 9 bounds how many Ranked Matches a pair may
   play, not how many points each one moves. With a margin term, a colluding
   donor who already knows the secret could tune the transfer size per Match.
3. Remaining Score mostly measures how many Clues were bought. R-T-05.4 already
   refuses to rank by Score for exactly that reason: ranking by leftover Score
   rewards not playing.

## 3. The two edges

### 3.1 Player versus Player

Expected score uses the continuous logistic form with denominator `400` and no
cap on the rating difference:

```text
E_A = 1 / (1 + 10^((R_B - R_A) / 400))
T   = round_half_away_from_zero(32 * (S_A - E_A))
R'_A = R_A + T
R'_B = R_B - T
```

`A` is the Player Identity that sorts first under a fixed canonical order, so
`T` is computed exactly once from one floating-point evaluation. Because the
same `T` is applied as `+T` and `-T`, `delta_A + delta_B = 0` holds by
construction, independent of the rounding rule. This is the invariant form
recommended by the Elo integrity research, §1.3.

No rating-difference cap is applied. Section 4 holds the Bot Opponent edge
inside `500..1500`, which keeps realised differences far below the point where
a cap would bite, so a cap would add a versioned parameter without buying an
invariant.

Applied transfers, from the higher-rated side's perspective:

| Rating difference | Higher-rated wins | Draw | Higher-rated loses |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| `0` | `+16` | `0` | `-16` |
| `200` | `+8` | `-8` | `-24` |
| `1000` | `0` | `-16` | `-32` |

The `0` in the top-right cell is a real consequence of integer storage: at an
extreme difference the favourite gains nothing for winning. This is accepted in
exchange for section 3.3.

### 3.2 Player versus Bot Opponent

A Bot Opponent holds no rating, so `E` cannot be computed; it is asserted
structurally. The profile calibrates the Bot Opponent to the Player's own
Ranked Rating, therefore `E = 0.5` always, `K = 32`, and the Player's rating
changes one-sidedly. Inside the calibrated domain the applied delta is `+16` on
a win, `0` on a Draw, and `-16` on a loss; section 4 gives the full table,
including the saturated cases outside the domain.

This yields an invariant worth stating explicitly, because it unifies this
ticket's convergence criterion with the profile's validation criterion:

```text
E[dR] = K * (P(win) + 0.5 * P(draw) - 0.5) = (K / 2) * (P(win) - P(loss))
```

so expected drift is zero exactly when `P(win) = P(loss)`, that is when the
decisive share `P(win) / (P(win) + P(loss))` is 50%. Decisive share, with Draw
reported separately, is precisely the quantity
`digitcode-bot-calibration/1.0.0` already uses for live validation across
eleven Rating bands. The rating converges when, and only when, the profile is
calibrated. Section 2 is what makes these the same number; a margin term would
separate them.

`K` stays at `32` on this edge rather than being lowered. The calibration
function is coarsely quantised — the target Bot Score is `clamp(floor((R - 500)
/ 25), 0, 40)`, and the whole domain `500..1500` holds only 45 distinct
`(target, inference budget)` pairs. Inside one 25-point band the Bot Opponent
is identical, so the restoring force is zero there. At `K = 32` a decisive
result moves `16`, so two decisive results cross a band. A smaller `K` would
weaken the restoring force proportionally, and that restoring force is the
whole mechanism by which a Ranked Rating settles at true strength.

### 3.3 Precision

Ranked Rating is an **integer**, and it is the same number when stored, when
displayed, and when read by the Bot Calibration Profile. This is forced from
two directions. The profile's offline verification enumerates the 1,001 integer
Ratings of `500..1500`. The opponent-model decision Q15 requires the Bot
Opponent to track the *displayed* number, because tracking any other number
destroys the self-correcting property. Storing two decimals and displaying a
rounded integer would make the tracked number differ from the shown number.

Transfers below `0.5` in magnitude therefore quantise to zero and are lost.
That loss is accepted.

## 4. Saturation outside the calibrated domain

`digitcode-bot-calibration/1.0.0` requires this ticket to choose a cap or an
equivalent saturation semantic, and forbids presenting a Ranked Rating outside
`500..1500` as still calibrated.

The scale is not self-bounding. The opponent-model decision Q15 argued that the
top of the scale bounds itself because the strongest Bot Opponent is
unbeatable. Profile `1.0.0` does not support that: it holds every Rating band,
`1500` included, to a decisive share inside `40%..60%`, so the strongest
calibrated Bot Opponent is meant to lose roughly half of its decisive Matches,
and the profile states outright that its results do not prove the strongest Bot
Opponent unbeatable. Above `1500` the Bot Opponent stops getting stronger while
a stronger Player keeps winning, so without an explicit rule the rating climbs
without bound. The low end is self-evacuating for the mirror-image reason:
below `500` the Bot Opponent stops getting weaker, the Player nearly always
wins or draws, and the rating is pushed back up.

**Decision: directional saturation on the Bot Opponent edge only. The stored
Ranked Rating is never clamped, floored, or capped.** Using the same Match
Start snapshot the profile already stamps:

| Stamped snapshot | Player wins | Draw | Player loses |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| `500 <= R <= 1500` | `+16` | `0` | `-16` |
| `R > 1500` | `0` | `0` | `-16` |
| `R < 500` | `+16` | `0` | `0` |

The Player-versus-Player edge is untouched: no floor, no ceiling, `+T/-T`
always.

Four reasons:

1. A cap on the stored number would reach the zero-sum edge, where a capped
   winner gains `0` while the loser still pays, breaking `delta_A + delta_B =
   0`. Elo integrity research §1.5 lists a one-sided floor as one of six ways
   to lose zero-sum. Directional saturation places the entire bounding
   mechanism on the edge that is already one-sided by design, which is the only
   place in the system where there is nothing to break.
2. Freezing the Bot Opponent edge outside the domain would make an overshoot
   absorbing: a Player who crossed `1500` on one lucky Match could never be
   moved by a Bot Opponent again, and the human pool is usually empty.
3. Directional saturation makes `1500` a reflecting barrier. At `1516`, a
   Player of true `1500` strength wins half of their decisive Matches (`0`) and
   loses half (`-16`), drifting `-8` per decisive Match; even a Player winning
   80% of their decisive Matches drifts `-3.2`. Draws dilute both figures per
   Match without changing their sign. Every true strength at or above `1500`
   therefore clusters at the top of the domain. The scale loses resolution
   there, and says so, instead of pretending to keep measuring.
4. Neither direction opens a farming path: above `1500` no points can be won
   from a Bot Opponent, below `500` none can be lost to one.

Two consequences are recorded deliberately. First, removing the floor does not
in practice admit a negative Ranked Rating. Dumping across the Player-versus-
Player edge decays the same way boosting does — section 9 reason 3 — so a
single booster cannot take a donor below about `640`, and an unlimited supply
of fresh boosters at `1000` stops at about `280`, because the transfer
quantises to `0` once the gap passes `719`. Going lower would need a
deliberately assembled chain of already-low accounts, and section 4's low-end
rule lifts every one of them the moment it plays a Ranked Match. In exchange
for dropping the floor, `delta_A + delta_B = 0` holds without exception.
Second, the lowest bands will not reach `VALIDATED` under the profile's
`40%..60%` criterion, because decisive share there tends toward 100%. That is
correct behaviour: below `500` is an exit region, not a measurement region, and
the profile's criterion must be read as applying to the calibrated domain.

## 5. The measurement phase, and what Skill Estimate is for

Skill Estimate had no remaining consumer. The opponent-model decision Q15 moved
Bot Opponent calibration onto Ranked Rating and explicitly rejected tracking
Skill Estimate; the Queue lifecycle decision settled that the matcher selects
the oldest eligible Queue Entry with a stable server tiebreak and uses no
rating at all. Neither `bot-calibration-profile.md` nor
`matchmaking-queue-lifecycle.md` mentions Skill Estimate. Its only remaining
job is the seeding rule of the opponent-model decision Q17.

**Decision: Skill Estimate is the pre-Ranked measurement phase, and nothing
else.**

- It **starts at `1000`**, the same baseline a Ranked Rating would otherwise
  have had.
- It runs on the same yardstick as Ranked Rating: integer, `E = 0.5`, the
  saturation rules of section 4 — but with `K_seed = 128`, so each decisive
  result moves `64`.
- It is updated **only** by a Practice Match against a Bot Opponent. Practice
  Matches against a Player never touch it.
- Ranked eligibility requires `N = 5` completed Practice Matches against a Bot
  Opponent.
- At the Player's first Ranked Match, `Ranked Rating := Skill Estimate`.
  Thereafter Ranked Rating runs at `K = 32` and Skill Estimate is frozen.

Reasons:

1. The real problem is the starting point, not the speed. A Player of true
   `600` strength starting at `1000` meets a Bot Opponent with an eleven-Clue
   inference budget, which solves about 40% of Ranked Puzzles. Their drift is
   roughly `-6.4` per Match, so about 62 Ranked Matches of up to fifteen
   minutes each are needed to reach their true level. A measurement Match moves
   four times as far per decisive result, closing the same distance in roughly
   a quarter of the Matches. Five of them do not finish the job: their movement
   is bounded at `±320`, and the expected movement is smaller still, because a
   draw contributes nothing. What five buys is that most of the distance is
   gone before any Ranked Rating exists, and the rest is walked at `K = 32`
   from a far better starting point. Five is a head start, not a guarantee of
   arrival.
2. The measurement phase cannot be farmed, for the same reason the whole
   opponent-model design works: seeding to `1500` requires genuinely beating
   Bot Opponents at the `1500` level, and the Bot Opponent rises with the seed.
   Sandbagging down to `500` is equally pointless, because the Bot Opponent
   there is weak and the seed climbs again. A large `K_seed` shortens the
   measurement; it does not open a path.
3. Restricting the input to Bot Opponent Practice Matches satisfies the
   opponent-model decision Q17 structurally rather than by policy. A Practice
   Room is invite-only and, per the identity decision Q11.4, uncapped — no
   cooldown, no pool threshold, roughly fifteen seconds per Match. If human
   Practice Matches fed Skill Estimate, that path would lead straight into
   Ranked Rating.
4. It avoids inventing a second rating system with its own `K`, ledger,
   corrections, and audit surface for a number nobody reads.

No provisional `K` exists on the Ranked side, and per-player `K` is never used.
Every Ranked Match uses `K = 32` for both sides.

Raising the identity decision Q10(ii) from one completed Practice Match to five
is within scope: the Queue lifecycle decision assigned Ranked eligibility to
this ticket. The requirement stops being friction and becomes a measurement
with a purpose.

One bias is accepted with open eyes. Practice draws from the full pool of
465,120 secrets (R-P-09) while Ranked draws from 464,948 after removing the 172
collision secrets (R-P-10), and the solver was verified only on the Ranked
pool. On a collision Puzzle the Bot Opponent never reaches a single remaining
candidate, so it never submits and expires, which tilts `172 / 465,120` of
measurement Matches toward the Player. Correcting this by changing the Practice
pool would change R-P-09. The Ruleset's own tables classify neither pool rule
as configurable, so that is a specification change rather than a parameter
change, and under R-K-04 it would make Matches on either side of it
incomparable — ending the Rating Generation of section 8. That is not worth
0.037%.

## 6. Ranked eligibility

`ENQUEUE_RANKED` is admitted only when the Player Identity satisfies all three:

1. it has **ever** linked a Google Linked Identity, recorded as a durable
   property rather than a live provider query;
2. it has completed **five** Practice Matches against a Bot Opponent in the
   current Rating Generation (sections 5 and 8);
3. it is not an anonymous-only Player Identity.

The check runs at admission and again inside the Match Start transaction,
because a Queue Entry may wait until `expires_at`.

**Losing or unlinking Google does not revoke Ranked eligibility.** The
access-loss decision handed this ticket the question of future Ranked
eligibility after Google access is lost; the answer is that eligibility
persists, and a linked email that self-recovers the Player Identity resumes
Ranked play with the same Ranked Rating and history.

Reasons:

1. It answers the access-loss decision's question without touching anything the
   access-loss decision settled. Requiring a currently linked Google would
   force a new guardrail around unlinking, cutting across the `2 -> 1`
   transition the access-loss decision permits after recent authentication.
2. What Google buys is an entry gate, not a standing state. A boosting ring
   pays one Google account per donor at entry, and that cost is unchanged
   either way. A live check adds no cost to an attacker and penalises only the
   Player who lost an account.
3. The capacity argument behind the identity decision's Google requirement — a
   project-wide limit of six OTPs per hour — constrains sign-in volume, and
   every new Player still enters through Google. Players who lost Google are a
   small tail.
4. A live check would create exactly the state the access-loss decision
   removed: a Player who self-recovers successfully, sees their own Ranked
   Rating and history, and is then barred from queueing.

This completes the funnel of the opponent-model decision Q14: an anonymous
Player plays, runs the five measurement Matches, links Google, and carries the
seed into their first Ranked Match. Losing an anonymous session before linking
remains irreversible per the access-loss decision, and the concrete loss is the
measurement phase: five Matches must be replayed from `1000`.

## 7. Rating eligibility

**Every finalized Ranked Match is rating-eligible. There is no void branch
reachable from gameplay.**

Most of the nine-row matrix left open by the Elo integrity research §2.2 no
longer exists. No-show and expired-invite cases cannot occur on the Ranked
path, because assignment, Queue Entry consumption, and Match Start are one
atomic transaction with no durable intermediate phase. Cancellation after start
is forbidden by R-T-08. Disconnection is not a distinct terminal reason: the
clock runs on (R-S-04) and an absent Player expires (R-T-07), which also states
that rage-quit and network loss MUST NOT be distinguished.

| Terminal combination | Outcome | Rated | `(S_player, S_opponent)` |
| --- | --- | :---: | --- |
| Both `SOLVED` | Score or Bot Score, then Solve Time, else Draw | yes | `1/0`, `0/1`, or `0.5/0.5` |
| Exactly one `SOLVED` | That side wins | yes | `1/0` or `0/1` |
| Neither `SOLVED`, exactly one `FORFEITED` | The Opponent wins | yes | `1/0` or `0/1` |
| Neither `SOLVED`, anything else, including **both** `FORFEITED` | Draw | yes | `0.5/0.5` |

Reasons:

1. A void branch is an attack surface, not a safety valve: any condition that
   makes a Match not count becomes an escape from a loss. The Match lifecycle
   decision settled that every invariant must be held by a constraint, because
   the web API is a control point and not a trust boundary, and no constraint
   can hold a "this one does not count" flag.
2. A platform failure cannot be separated from a disconnection under the rules.
   If the database or realtime layer fails mid-Match, the clock still runs,
   both sides expire, and R-T-05.4 yields a Draw. Treating that as void would
   require the distinction R-T-07 forbids. The cost is low: on the Bot Opponent
   edge a Draw is `0`.
3. `FORFEITED` is always rated. R-T-06 makes a Forfeit a loss regardless of the
   Opponent's state, and R-T-04 ranks it last precisely to close the
   forced-draw path.

**R-T-05 is total and complementary.** Every terminal combination produces
exactly one outcome, and no combination produces a `0/0` or `1/1` score pair —
even a mutual Forfeit is a Draw. The trap described in the Elo integrity
research §1.5, where `0/0` destroys `K` points and `1/1` creates `K`, cannot
occur in DigitCode. `S_A + S_B = 1` on every Player-versus-Player Ranked Match
is a consequence of the Ruleset, not a rule that needs defending.

## 8. Rating Generations

R-K-04 states that two Matches are comparable only under the same `ruleset_id`,
and the game specification hands this ticket that constraint as a hard input.
It is heavier than it looks: `digitcode-bot-calibration/1.0.0` binds exactly
one Ruleset, and the whole Ranked scale is anchored to that yardstick. Bumping
the Ruleset invalidates the profile, which changes the yardstick, so no number
survives the bump.

**A Ranked Rating belongs to a Rating Generation, stamped with the triple
`(ruleset_id, profile_id, formula_version)`.** `formula_version` names the
arithmetic of section 3, so that changing the expectation function, `K`, or the
quantisation rule ends a Generation for the same reason changing the Ruleset or
the profile does: the yardstick moved. On a bump of any of the three:

- Matches started before the cutover settle normally into the old Generation.
  There is no overlap case, because the Queue lifecycle decision forbids
  starting a new Match before the previous one is finalized.
- The old Generation's rating is archived with its Generation label. It is
  neither deleted nor carried forward.
- Ranked eligibility lapses until the new measurement is complete, because the
  condition in section 6 counts Practice Matches in the current Generation. A
  Player has no Ranked Rating in a Generation until one has been seeded.
- The new Generation reuses the measurement phase of section 5: Skill Estimate
  is unfrozen and initialised to the Player's final Ranked Rating in the
  previous Generation — not to `1000`, since the old number is still a good
  prior even though it is no longer a measurement — then five Practice Matches
  against a Bot Opponent run at `K_seed = 128` under the new yardstick, and
  their result seeds the new Ranked Rating.

This honours R-K-04 exactly: no number is ever claimed to be comparable across
two yardsticks. It introduces no new mechanism, only a second use of one that
already exists. A live validation failure in some Rating band is handled the
same way, because the profile forbids auto-tuning a published profile: the
response is a new profile version, which is a new Generation.

## 9. Matching policy inputs

`matchmaking-queue-lifecycle.md` §1 names five policy inputs the Queue consumes
without defining. Ranked eligibility is answered in section 6; the other four
are answered here.

A gap has to be named first. The Queue lifecycle decision settles that the
matcher selects the oldest eligible Queue Entry, which chooses the **anchor**.
If the anchor's **counterpart** were simply the next-oldest entry, two accounts
enqueueing in the same instant would be the two oldest entries and would be
paired deterministically, regardless of pool size. A pool threshold alone
cannot close that. This is the piece the Queue lifecycle decision named
"counterpart eligibility" and left here.

| Input | Value |
| --- | --- |
| `bot_eligible_at` | `enqueue + 15 seconds` |
| Counterpart selection | **uniformly at random** among eligible waiting Queue Entries, not next-oldest |
| Pool threshold | human-versus-human only when at least **4** eligible Queue Entries are waiting |
| Pair cooldown | **6-hour blocks** on fixed UTC boundaries, held by a uniqueness constraint over the unordered Player Identity pair and the block number; not applied to Player–Bot pairs |

`expires_at = bot_eligible_at + 60 seconds` was already settled by the Queue
lifecycle decision.

Reasons:

1. Uniform random selection is what makes a pool threshold mean anything. The
   anchor stays the oldest entry, so nobody is starved and the waiting order
   still has force, but a colluding pair now meets with probability at most
   `1/3` at the chosen threshold. It grants no Player any preference, so
   `ENQUEUE_RANKED` remains advance consent to a server-assigned Opponent.
2. The threshold is `4` rather than something large because the threshold
   counts waiting entries, and an attacker can supply waiting entries. With
   enough of their own accounts queued, any threshold is met and randomisation
   mostly pairs them with each other. A high threshold buys little real safety
   and removes what little human-versus-human play a small population has. What
   actually prices the attack is the per-donor cost: one Google account
   (section 6) plus five Practice Matches against a Bot Opponent (section 5),
   up to roughly 75 minutes per donor before that donor can queue for Ranked
   even once. The measurement phase turns out to be the most expensive Sybil
   barrier in the design, and it is there for an unrelated reason.
3. A 6-hour block is nearly free. In a small population honest Players will
   meet repeatedly, but the practical consequence of being blocked is being
   paired with a Bot Opponent, which is the design's default mode anyway.
   Nobody is prevented from playing. What the block buys is better than a
   points-per-day figure suggests, because zero-sum makes each transfer widen
   the gap by twice its own size, so the transfers decay. From `1000/1000` the
   first Match moves `16`, the tenth about `7`, the fiftieth `2`; once the gap
   passes `719` the transfer quantises to `0`. A single donor therefore cannot
   push a booster past about `1360` — `1500` is unreachable — and reaching even
   that takes 156 Matches, which at one Match per pair per block is about 39
   days. An unlimited supply of fresh donors at `1000` moves the wall only to
   about `1720`. The ceiling is structural, from zero-sum plus integer
   quantisation; the block supplies the time. Low human-versus-human volume is
   acceptable by design, because the opponent-model decision Q2 moved the
   evidence from a connectivity graph to a shared yardstick.
4. Fixed UTC boundaries make the block number `floor(epoch_seconds / 21600)`, a
   deterministic expression computable inside an index. The Match lifecycle
   decision requires invariants to be held by constraints; a sliding window
   would be a `COUNT` under a lock. Clustering at a block boundary — a pair
   playing at `05:59` and again at `06:01` — was identified and accepted by the
   opponent-model decision: at most two back-to-back Matches, with no change to
   the long-run rate.

The cooldown applies to Player Identity pairs, so signing out and back in does
not reset a block.

## 10. Settlement

**The race between two different Matches touching the same Player does not
exist in this system.** The Queue lifecycle decision forbids a Player from
starting a new Match before the previous one is finalized, so at any instant
the set of unfinalized Matches is a set of pairwise-disjoint pairs and no
Player Identity appears twice. Two concurrent settlements therefore cannot
touch the same rating row, and the machinery the Elo integrity research §5.2
proposes for that race — ordered row locks or Serializable with retry — is not
required for correctness.

**Settlement order does not affect the result either.** Expected score is
computed from the Ranked Rating snapshot stamped at Match Start, not from the
current rating read at settlement time. Because `T` is therefore fixed at Match
Start, settlement is the addition of a constant, and addition commutes. The
order-dependence counterexample of research §5.3 assumes `E` is evaluated at
settlement; it does not apply. This ticket consequently does not need to choose
a canonical total order for rating events.

One snapshot serves three purposes: Bot Opponent calibration (already stamped
by the profile), the saturation domain test of section 4, and expected score
here.

A correction committed between Match Start and settlement is therefore the only
case in which a stamped snapshot differs from the current rating. That is
deliberate and it is safe: the running Match settles on the number it was
started under, and the correction's own delta is applied on top. Neither is
lost, and both are visible in the Ledger.

Both conclusions rest on the one-unfinalized-Match rule being held by a
constraint — at most one unfinalized Match per Player Identity — and not by an
application-level check. The exact index is schema, and belongs to its owners
in section 13; what this ticket fixes is that a constraint, not a code path,
holds it. This ticket declares that dependency; implementing it belongs to
[Chốt data model, lịch sử đấu và quyền riêng
tư](https://github.com/tdhcoding/DigitCode-EscapeRoom/issues/14) and [Chọn kiến
trúc web và managed
services](https://github.com/tdhcoding/DigitCode-EscapeRoom/issues/3).

Contract:

- **Rating Settlement happens inside the finalization transaction**, not in a
  follow-up job. The Queue lifecycle decision already settles that the
  transition making the second side terminal also fixes outcome, result, and
  version. The hard reason is that [Nghiên cứu cơ chế scheduled job
  zero-cost](https://github.com/tdhcoding/DigitCode-EscapeRoom/issues/27)
proved no zero-cost scheduled mechanism is dependable enough for a Match to
rely on, so there must be no "settle later" step. It also avoids the
two-datastore problem, so no outbox or inbox is needed.
- **The idempotency key of the initial settlement is `match_id`**, held by a
  uniqueness constraint admitting at most one initial event per Match. A retry
  returns the committed event and never recomputes.
- **There is no payload that can disagree.** The settlement command takes only
  `match_id`; every input — both rating snapshots, `K`, the outcome, the
  Generation, the saturation flag — is read from the Match row stamped at Match
  Start. The "same key, different parameters" failure class is therefore not
  representable, which is stronger and cheaper than detecting it.
- **One atomic transaction** appends one Rating Ledger event, updates both
  ratings (one on the Bot Opponent edge), marks the Match settled, and fixes
  outcome, result, and version. Success is published only after commit.
- Both rating rows are still locked in a fixed Player Identity order. Against
  another settlement that is defence in depth, turning an admission-layer bug
  into contention rather than a wrong rating; against an operator correction,
  which the disjointness argument does not cover, it is load-bearing. Section
  11 relies on it.

## 11. Rating Ledger and corrections

**Corrections are forward, append-only adjustments. Replay is rejected because
it is undefined here, not because it is expensive.**

In this system a rating does not merely record results; it *generates the
Opponent*. The Bot Calibration Profile reads the stamped Ranked Rating snapshot
to choose the target Bot Score and inference budget. Changing a past rating
means a later Match should have faced a different Bot Opponent, so the
counterfactual result does not exist — nobody knows how that Player would have
played against a Bot Opponent that never appeared. Chess can replay because
results are fixed; here the result is a function of the rating.

The price of abandoning replay is far lower than research §7.2 implies, because
the dominant edge is immune to a wrong rating. On the Bot Opponent edge `E =
0.5` regardless of rating, so transfers are always `±16`: a wrong rating costs
nothing on subsequent Bot Opponent Matches. It propagates only through two
narrow channels — which Bot Opponent was faced, and which side of the `500` and
`1500` saturation boundaries the Player sat on — while the Player-versus-Player
edge, where `E` genuinely depends on the difference, is made sparse by the pair
cooldown of section 9.

Contract:

- The Rating Ledger is **strictly append-only**. Events are never modified or
  deleted. Mutate-and-delete is rejected outright: the research rates its audit
  quality as poor, and it makes idempotent retry ambiguous.
- A correction is a **new event** of kind `correction` referencing the
  superseded event, carrying actor, reason, and an explicit delta. On the
  Player-versus-Player edge it is also a single quantity applied as `+X/-X`, so
  zero-sum survives corrections — closing the sixth item of research §1.5.
- Corrections take effect **immediately**, with no quarantine state.
  Enforcement is already human-reviewed per [Chốt threat model và anti-cheat
  boundary](https://github.com/tdhcoding/DigitCode-EscapeRoom/issues/12).
- **A correction adjusts rating and never reverses a Match result.** The Match
  row, terminal states, outcome, and Solve Times are immutable: R-T-08 forbids
  cancelling a Match, and the access-loss decision settled that losing access
  reverses no Match result, no Ranked Rating change, and no settlement. Neither
  source says a result can never be reversed by anything; that a **correction**
  does not reverse one is decided here. Match history shows the original
  result; the Ledger shows the original transfer and the correction. Both are
  true, and they answer different questions.
- Corrections are themselves idempotent, under their own key and their own
  uniqueness constraint.
- Only an operator may create a correction.
- **A correction serialises against settlement.** A correction runs in its own
  transaction and takes the same rating rows in the same canonical Player
  Identity order that section 10 uses, so a correction and a Rating Settlement
  can never interleave on one rating. This is the one place the ordered lock of
  section 10 is load-bearing rather than defensive: the disjointness argument
  covers settlement against settlement, and an operator correction is outside
  it.

Minimum event fields:

| Group | Fields |
| --- | --- |
| Identity | immutable `event_id`, `match_id`, `event_kind`, `edge` (`pvp` or `bot`) |
| Generation | the Rating Generation triple: `ruleset_id`, `profile_id`, `formula_version` |
| Sides | both Player Identities, or Player Identity and `profile_id` |
| Result | both terminal states, canonical outcome, the pair `(S_A, S_B)` |
| Arithmetic | both stamped rating snapshots, `E_A`, `K`, raw transfer, **applied** transfer, resulting ratings, saturation flag applied |
| Operations | idempotency key, commit time; for a correction: superseded `event_id`, actor, reason |

**Reconciliation.** Because settlement order does not matter, a Player's
current rating in a Generation equals their section 5 seed plus the sum of
applied deltas of every event in that Generation. No ordered rebuild and no
intermediate snapshots are required. Any divergence between the materialised
rating and that sum is an error, detectable by a single query.

## 12. Acceptance invariants

For the later specification and execution tickets:

1. A Ranked Match has at most one initial Rating Settlement effect, however
   many deliveries occur.
2. A retry carrying the same `match_id` returns the committed event and changes
   no rating.
3. Both rating updates and the Ledger event commit together or not at all.
4. No two concurrent settlements touch the same Player Identity, because the
   set of unfinalized Matches is pairwise disjoint, held by a constraint.
5. Practice Matches and non-Ranked Matches never produce a Ranked Rating
   change.
6. Every Player-versus-Player rated Match satisfies `S_A + S_B = 1` and
   `delta_A + delta_B = 0` after quantisation.
7. Bot Opponent edge deltas are one-sided and are drawn from `{+16, 0, -16}`,
   restricted by section 4 when the stamped snapshot lies outside `500..1500`.
8. Ranked Rating is an integer, identical when stored, displayed, and read by
   the Bot Calibration Profile.
9. No rating arithmetic reads Score, Bot Score, or Solve Time.
10. A Ranked Rating is never compared across Rating Generations.
11. Current rating equals seed plus the sum of applied deltas within a
    Generation; reconciliation detects any divergence.
12. A correction never mutates an existing event and never alters a Match
    result.
13. Concurrent duplicates, timeouts after commit, and lost responses cannot
    apply a transfer twice.
14. A correction and a Rating Settlement never interleave on one rating, and a
    Match settles on the snapshot stamped at its own Match Start even if a
    correction lands first.
